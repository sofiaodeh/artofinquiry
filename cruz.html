Episode 10

Pedro M. Cruz

Giving Data a Body

Pedro M. Cruz started out studying engineering physics in Portugal, wanting to see the concepts behind the equations, not just memorize the steps to solve them. Writing his first physics simulations, and watching electric field lines move on his screen, led him to informatics engineering and then to design. He is now an associate professor of Art and Design at Northeastern University, where he teaches information design and data visualization and co-directs the CoLab for Data Impact.

We talk about where data ends and meaning begins: why four colonial empires bump into each other in one of his animations, how two centuries of US immigration became the rings of a tree, why Lisbon’s traffic is colored like traffic lights, and why politicians who move between corporate boards became swarming creatures somewhere between a bug and a slug.

The work we
talked about

Visualizing Empires Decline

The Portuguese, Spanish, French, and British empires shrink over time as their colonies become independent. The land areas come from data. The way the empires bump into each other as they shrink does not. Pedro added it because the empires were always competing for space. As he puts it in the episode, that part is not data, it is meaning.

Simulated Dendrochronology of US Immigration

Two centuries of immigration to the United States grow outward as the rings of a tree, with one cell for every hundred people. Partway through the project, Pedro learned that the cells at the center of a real tree are dead but still part of its structure. The oldest rings became the people who arrived long ago and are still part of what makes the country.

Lisbon’s traffic

A day of traffic in Lisbon, colored like traffic lights so anyone can read the speed at a glance. Watching it, you can see the city wake up in the morning and go home at night.

An Ecosystem of Corporate Politicians

Portuguese politicians who move between government and corporate boards, drawn as swarming creatures somewhere between a bug and a slug. Pedro took the idea from old political caricatures, which have always used animals to say something about the people they draw.

See more of Pedro’s work at pmcruz.com.

In the classroom

Learning the rules
before breaking them

Pedro teaches his students the rules of visualization first, and only then shows them work that breaks those rules on purpose. Breaking a rule is fine, as long as you know which line you are crossing and why.
